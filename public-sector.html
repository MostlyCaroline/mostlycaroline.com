Working with, not in

Years working with local government, never inside it. Being the outside agency, not a council employee, meant I could ask the question a permanent team member couldn't always risk asking - and that changed what actually got built.

Client
Local Government
My Role
Lead Designer
Year
2006 - 2016
Platform
Responsive Web · Recruitment System & CMS

Situation

On the outside of local government

At Abacus, I worked on Webstructure, the CMS platform behind local authority websites across the UK. I sat one step removed from each council - close enough to understand the work, but never bound by the internal governance, sign-off processes and regulatory compliance that shaped how their own teams could operate.

Users

Residents, jobseekers and council employees trying to get something done online - often when they were busy, under pressure or dealing with something important. The website wasn't the thing they came for. It was the thing they had to get through.

Stakeholders

Council officers balancing what people needed with what the organisation could approve, fund, build and support - while answering to legal, communications, technical and governance teams.

Insight

Distance was an advantage, not a gap

Working with government, not in it, meant I wasn't the one who had to defend last year's decision, or protect a relationship with the compliance team. I hadn't signed anything off, so I had nothing to lose by questioning it. That distance was often exactly what let an awkward question get asked, and actually heard, in a way it might not have been from inside the building.

Task

Use the outsider's licence, don't waste it

My job was to bring a view the internal team couldn't always afford to bring themselves - including questioning whether "compliant" accessibility work actually worked, and whether an assumed audience had ever really been asked what they needed.

Action

Three questions an insider might not have asked

"Is this form actually usable, or just compliant?" Auditing a set of third-party forms, I found a key one couldn't be completed using a keyboard alone. It had presumably been signed off by someone internally already - but I hadn't been the one to sign it off, so flagging it wasn't a criticism of my own work, or anyone I had to keep working alongside indefinitely. I worked with the developer to fix the interaction itself: controls reachable and operable in a logical sequence, with visible focus at every step, addressing the Keyboard and Focus Order success criteria still core to WCAG today.

"Have you actually asked them?" Running workshops with groups of teenagers for Lincolnshire County Council's new children's services website, I could tell the client directly that what they'd assumed about their own users didn't hold up - without it costing me a reporting line or a future performance review. What the teenagers told us reshaped the approach entirely.

"Why does this take two weeks?" At Derbyshire County Council, recruitment ran entirely on paper: more than 60,000 applicant packs sent out every year, at over £2 each, with roughly a two-week gap between a vacancy going live and an application reaching a manager. Nobody inside had seriously questioned whether that was fixable - it was just how recruitment worked. I led UX and UI to implement the system to replace it.

Result

Being outside made the question easier. Being good made it land.

  • The keyboard and focus-order fix got made, rather than waved through on existing sign-off.
  • Workshop findings directly reshaped the children's services site before launch, rather than being retrofitted after complaints.
  • Lincolnshire County Council's website went on to win Government Website of the Year.
  • At Derbyshire, redesigning the recruitment flow saved close to £190,000 a year - £100,000 from cutting candidate-pack costs, £90,000 from eliminating manual re-keying - and took 60% of the council's 5,000 annual vacancies fully online.

Reflection

The outsider's licence has a limit

Being the outsider gave me permission to ask questions that might otherwise have gone unasked. But it also gave me a responsibility: if I challenged a decision, I needed to understand what it would take to change it, not just point out that it was wrong.

The trade-off was that I could leave. The people I was challenging couldn't. They still had to get the service through governance, budget, procurement, legal and political scrutiny - and live with the consequences after I had moved on to the next project.

Looking back, I would do more to understand those constraints before pushing against them. Not to make the challenge softer, but to make it more useful. I'd spend more time understanding who needed to be convinced, what evidence they needed, and what could realistically change within the organisation.

That is probably the biggest thing this work taught me about stakeholder practice: being the person who asks the difficult question is only half the job. The other half is helping people feel safe enough - and equipped enough - to do something about the answer.

I still value the outsider's perspective. I just understand now that good stakeholder practice isn't about having the freedom to challenge. It's about using that freedom responsibly.
