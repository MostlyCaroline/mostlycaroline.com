UX Case Study · Behaviour Change · Mobile App

Designed for the days that don't go to plan

A behaviour change feature for healthcare client PrimeCare, built around one idea: don't punish people for missing a day.

Client
PrimeCare
My Role
Product Designer
Duration
Feb - July 2026
Platform
Mobile App

Situation

Habit apps punish bad days

PrimeCare needed a digital product to help patients build sustainable health habits. Research quickly surfaced why most habit-tracking apps fail: a single missed day gets treated as failure, with no distinction between "life got in the way" and "gave up." Every patient who abandons the app after one bad day is a habit that never gets the chance to stick.

"I gave up after missing two days. There didn't seem to be any point carrying on."- Research participant
Users

Patients managing long-term health conditions, enrolled in a PrimeCare care programme and tracking daily habits as part of it.

Stakeholders

PrimeCare's clinical team, who review engagement patterns to spot when a patient may need follow-up support.

The app isn't the whole service. A run of Life Days in a row is also a signal PrimeCare's care team can act on - not just a stat on a dashboard.

Insight

A bad day and giving up looked identical

Interviews and competitive analysis kept surfacing the same three patterns: streaks punished any missed day, progress felt invisible next to what had been missed, and no product accounted for real life getting in the way. Reframing the job to be done - protect progress, don't police it - pointed straight at three questions.

  1. HMWmake a missed day feel different from giving up?
  2. HMWmake progress visible, not just what's been missed?
  3. HMWaccommodate real life without diluting the habit?

Task

Build a tracker people keep using

Design a feature for PrimeCare's existing app that helps patients build lasting habits - without the shame-based mechanics that were driving people to quit. The target: WCAG 2.1 AA throughout, validated through real usability testing rather than assumption.

Action

Life Day - permission to be human

I led research, journey mapping and three rounds of usability testing. Interviews and competitive analysis confirmed streak-breaking as the single biggest trigger for app abandonment - which shaped the core feature: Life Day, a shield users can log instead of a missed day. Their streak holds. Their progress stays protected. Life Days are earned, not given - three per month for every ten consecutive days tracked.

Mid-fi usability testing on the feature surfaced seven issues, from users not understanding what a Life Day is to needing more prominent access to it - all resolved before the design moved into a full design system across 20+ hi-fi screens.

Result

Signed off, and tested well

Usability testing on the mid-fi prototype validated HabitHero's core flows and confirmed Life Day achieved exactly what the research called for. The design was signed off for development handoff via Figma Dev Mode.

"This actually makes me want to come back tomorrow."- Usability testing participant

Reflection

Research isn't a stage - it's a conversation you keep having

The idea of Life Day came directly from listening to people describe what actually happened when life got in the way. But once it existed as an interaction rather than an idea, different questions appeared. Did people understand what it was? Did they know when to use it? Did it feel like permission to continue, or another rule they had to manage?

That reinforced something I already believe about product design: research isn't a stage you complete before designing. It's a conversation you keep having with the people you're designing for.

I also wouldn't consider the current result proof that Life Day will change behaviour over the long term. The usability testing showed that people understood the mechanic and responded positively to it. It didn't tell me whether three Life Days per month, earned after ten consecutive days, is the right behavioural intervention over six months.

If this were going into live development, my next step would be to define what success looks like beyond usability - return rates, habit continuation, use of Life Days, and whether people who use them are more likely to come back rather than abandon the product.

The biggest lesson for me was that good product design isn't about getting the answer right first time. It's about creating enough opportunities to find out when you've got it wrong.
