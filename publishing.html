UX Case Study · Publishing & Media · Design Systems

One design system, an industry's worth of titles

A large proportion of UK and global publishing groups, each with a portfolio of titles and its own commercial pressures, needed a mobile experience that didn't fall apart depending on which brand you landed on. Rather than solving that separately for every client, I helped build a shared, responsive design system - designed once, adaptable everywhere - so each publisher could move faster without losing what made their brand distinct.

Situation

Many publishers, one inconsistent experience

At the start of the project, most client sites had been built and maintained separately, on ageing, inconsistent setups. As mobile use grew, that inconsistency became a real cost: readers got a different, often worse, experience depending on the device and the brand, and every new product took months to build from scratch.

Users

Readers and subscribers across each publishing brand, increasingly browsing on mobile rather than desktop.

Stakeholders

Client publishers across the portfolio, each with their own commercial priorities, and understandably wary of losing brand identity to a shared system.

A one-off redesign wouldn't hold. Whatever we built had to run as a single CMS across publishers with different priorities and distinct brand identities, without turning into a bespoke product for each one.

Insight

Rebuilding from scratch was the real cost

Traffic data across the client base told the same story everywhere: mobile visits were closing in on desktop, but the experience on mobile was consistently worse. At the same time, every new client product meant starting again from zero - new patterns, new QA, months of lead time. The two problems pointed to one answer.

  1. HMWgive every brand a consistent, accessible experience without flattening what makes each one distinct?
  2. HMWmake it faster to launch new products without cutting corners on quality?
  3. HMWget client stakeholders to trust a shared system as much as their own bespoke one?

Task

Design once, reuse everywhere

As design lead, my job was to define a shared set of interface patterns that could flex across a wide range of publisher brands, get developers and QA aligned around building and maintaining them, and bring often-sceptical client stakeholders along with a shared system rather than a bespoke one each time.

Action

Patterns built once, adapted per brand

I progressed from junior designer to senior and then design lead over my time at Abacus, eventually mentoring other designers, front-end and QA team and acting as project manager on smaller projects. Rather than redesign each client site from scratch, I worked with developers to define a shared set of interface patterns - built once, adapted per client instead of rebuilt every time. That underpinned a responsive rollout across a wide range of major publishing brands, including EMAP titles like Retail Week and Health Service Journal, moving them onto one multichannel platform with a consistent experience across desktop, tablet and mobile.

Accessibility was a starting priority for the system and its templates, not an afterthought - work that crossed over directly with the WCAG accessibility fixes I was making on public sector sites during the same period. Alongside the client stakeholder workshops, I ran plenty of user and stakeholder workshops and usability testing with real users, across both the front end and the back end of the platform.

Result

A platform that could scale

The approach moved the business away from rebuilding every product from scratch.

  • A wide range of publishing brands moved onto a shared multichannel platform.
  • The reusable pattern approach created greater consistency across mobile, tablet and desktop.
  • Across the EMAP titles, mobile traffic shifted from roughly 50:50 to 80:20 compared with desktop.Source
  • Across the EMAP titles, paid content increased from 31% to 43% of revenue.Source

Reflection

A shared system only works when people believe it belongs to them

Looking back, I would have formalised the shared system earlier. We knew the commercial and delivery benefits of reusable patterns, but I underestimated how personal brand identity could be to publishing clients. To them, standardisation could sound like sameness - and sameness was exactly what they were paying us to avoid.

The lesson for me was that a design system isn't just a collection of reusable components. It's a negotiation between consistency and autonomy. The system has to make the common things easier without taking away the things that make a product recognisable.

If I were doing it again, I'd involve clients earlier in defining that boundary. Rather than presenting a finished system and asking them to trust it, I'd show them where standardisation was valuable, where flexibility was deliberate, and why both could exist together.

That changed how I think about design systems. The hardest part isn't designing something reusable. It's getting people to believe that reuse doesn't mean compromise.
