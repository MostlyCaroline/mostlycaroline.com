Overview

The brief: a habit tracking app that doesn't punish real life

PrimeCare wanted a digital product to support their patients in building sustainable health habits — tracking steps, distance, and calories across daily, weekly, and monthly views. The challenge wasn't building a tracker. It was building one that people would actually keep using.

Most habit apps create anxiety around missed days. Streaks break, motivation drops, users churn. PrimeCare needed something different — a product built around behaviour change principles that treated users as humans, not data points.

Research

What we learned about why habits fail

Before designing anything, I conducted user interviews and competitive analysis to understand why existing habit tracking products weren't working for PrimeCare's patient group — people managing long-term health conditions who needed sustainable behaviour change, not gamified pressure.

"I gave up after missing two days. There didn't seem to be any point carrying on." — Research participant

Key insight 1

Users felt punished for missing days. Broken streaks were the single biggest trigger for app abandonment.

Key insight 2

Progress felt invisible. Users couldn't see how far they'd come — only how much they'd missed.

Key insight 3

One-size tracking didn't fit. "Bad days" — illness, family, travel — weren't accommodated by any existing solution.

Key insight 4

Accessibility was largely ignored. Small touch targets, poor contrast, and dense data created barriers for older users.

The Key Design Decision

Life Day — the feature that changes everything

The single most important design decision in HabitHero was the Life Day feature. It emerged directly from research: users didn't need more motivation. They needed permission to be human.

A Life Day acts as a shield. When life gets in the way — illness, a difficult day, unexpected circumstances — a user can log a Life Day instead of a missed day. Their streak is preserved. Their progress is protected. They return to the app tomorrow instead of giving up entirely.

Life Days are earned, not given. Users receive 3 Life Days per month for every 10 consecutive days tracked — building responsibility alongside compassion.

Process

From discovery to validated prototype

1

Discovery & Research

User interviews, competitive analysis, and stakeholder sessions to define the problem space. Created a primary persona — Alex, a time-pressured professional managing a long-term health condition.

2

Synthesis & IA

Affinity mapping, How Might We questions, and information architecture to structure the app. Defined the core user flows — happy path, alternative path (Life Day), and retroactive logging.

User flow diagram
3

Lo-fi Wireframes

Paper prototypes and low-fidelity sketches to explore the core screens — onboarding, daily dashboard, weekly view, monthly calendar, and the Life Day flow — before committing to visual design.

Lo-fi wireframe sketches
4

Usability Testing — Round 1

Tested lo-fi prototype with 5 participants. Key findings: the Life Day concept needed clearer explanation; the leaderboard was motivating for some users but anxiety-inducing for others; onboarding felt too long.

5

Mid-fi Iteration

Iterated based on testing. Simplified onboarding to 3 screens. Redesigned the Life Day prompt to appear contextually — triggered by low activity, not by user request. Made leaderboard opt-in.

Mid-fi wireframes — daily view and Life Day flow
6

Design System

Built a structured design system in Figma covering typography, colour, spacing tokens, component states (default, active, disabled, error), and interaction patterns — maintaining consistency across all 20+ screens.

Design system components
7

Hi-fi Prototype & Final Testing

Two further rounds of usability testing on the high-fidelity prototype — including accessibility testing with users with visual impairments and motor difficulties. All flows validated to WCAG 2.1 AA standard.

Accessibility

Designed for everyone from the start

Accessibility wasn't a final checklist. It shaped every design decision — from touch target sizing (minimum 44px) to colour contrast ratios, from error state design to the language used throughout the app.

WCAG 2.1 AA

All text and interactive elements meet or exceed AA contrast requirements throughout the app.

Touch targets

Minimum 44px across all interactive elements, with generous spacing to prevent mis-taps.

Plain language

All copy written at a clear, accessible reading level — no jargon, no punishing language.

Error recovery

Clear, compassionate error states throughout — designed around how people actually behave, not how we wish they would.

Outcome

A validated product that respects real life

HabitHero delivered a tested, accessible, high-fidelity prototype ready for development handoff. Three rounds of usability testing validated the core user flows and confirmed that the Life Day feature — the product's defining differentiator — was both understood and valued by users.

Post-testing feedback showed that users felt the app was "on their side" — a significant shift from the punishing tone of competitor products.
